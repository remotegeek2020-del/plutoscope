Name & Origin Story 

Pluto is real. It has mountains, seasons, a thin nitrogen atmosphere, five moons — it just got reclassified as a 
“dwarf planet” in 2006  and quietly dropped out of the conversation most people have about the solar system. It 
didn't get smaller or less real. It got overlooked by a system that only has room for a handful of headline names.  

That's the exact position a solo creator or a small consulting shop is in today. The content is good, the expertise 
is real, but the visibility layer — Google's rankings, AI Overviews, ChatGPT's citations — was built around, and 
increasingly optimized for, a handful of headline brands. Being right isn't the same as being seen. 

A “scope” is the instrument that fixes that: a telescope, a periscope, an aperture that gathers enough light to 
bring something faint and far away into clear focus. Plutoscope is the instrument pointed at the part of the sky 
everyone else stopped looking at — the tool that finds and focuses on the sources AI engines are citing, ignoring, 
or getting wrong, across SEO, AEO, and GEO alike. 

The three disciplines map naturally onto the metaphor: SEO is the wide -field view (the whole visible sky of 
search results), AEO is zooming into a single bright point (the direct answer box), and GEO is deep -space 
observation — trying to see what's happening inside a process (an AI model synthesizing an answer) that doesn't 
show its work. Plutoscope is one instrument with three lenses, pointed at one problem: making sure something 
real doesn't stay invisible just because the system wasn't built to notice it. 

Draft Logo 

The mark: a scope ring (the instrument) with reticle ticks at the four compass points, a dashed orbital path 
suggesting motion and distance, and Pluto itself — small, warm-colored, caught in the ring, finally in focus. The 
off-center placement is deliberate: the subject isn't dead center by default, the instrument has to be pointed at 
it. 

Horizontal lockup (mark + wordmark), for headers, decks, and the app's top nav: 

Status: This is a first-pass concept to validate the direction (instrument + orbit + small-planet-in-focus), not a 
final production logo — worth a proper design pass (vector cleanup, favicon/app-icon crop, dark-mode 
variant) once the name is locked in. 

Color palette: deep indigo (#3B2E58) for the instrument/ring — trust, depth, the “serious tool” half of the brand. 
Warm amber (#E8A33D) for Pluto itself — visibility, warmth, the “finally seen” payoff. The pairing reads as cool 
instrument, warm subject, which mirrors the product's actual pitch: a precise measurement tool that exists to 
do something warm — get overlooked brands seen. 

Vision 

A future where visibility on the internet isn't rationed to whoever already has the biggest name — where being 
genuinely good at what you do is enough to be found, cited, and recommended by the search engines and AI 
systems people actually use, regardless of budget or brand size. 

Mission 

Plutoscope gives solo creators and independent consultants the same clarity into AI -era search visibility that, 
until now, only enterprise budgets could buy — one instrument that tracks, audits, and helps fix visibility across 
SEO, AEO, and GEO, so the people doing real work don't have to stay invisible just because the system wasn't 
pointed at them.

1. Executive  Summary 

Search is splitting into three overlapping arenas: traditional search engines (SEO), direct-answer surfaces like 
featured snippets and voice assistants (AEO), and AI chat and generative answer engines like ChatGPT, 
Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews (GEO). Over 60% of Google searches now end without a click to a third-
party site, and that share is growing as generative answers absorb more query volume. Brands that only track 
and optimize for classic SEO rankings are losing visibility they can't see or measure. 

This plan outlines a subscription platform that unifies all three disciplines in one product: visibility tracking 
across search and AI engines, automated content audits, AI-assisted content generation, and competitive 
citation analysis. The initial target is solo creators and marketing consultants who are underserved by today's 
enterprise-priced GEO tools, with a deliberate path to scale into agency and enterprise tiers as the product 
matures. 

The market has validated demand — tools like Profound, Peec AI, Otterly.AI, AthenaHQ, and Scrunch AI have 
emerged in the last two years — but pricing is bifurcated: entry tools ($12-$99/mo) offer thin feature sets, while 
capable platforms start at $189-$489/mo  and enterprise tools are priced out of reach for solo operators and 
small agencies. That gap is the opening. 

2. What SEO, AEO, and GEO Mean (and Why All Three  Matter) 

2.1 Definitions 

•  SEO (Search Engine Optimization): optimizing for rankings in traditional search results — keywords, 

backlinks, page speed, structured data. Goal: get clicked. 

•  AEO (Answer Engine Optimization): optimizing to be pulled into a direct answer — featured snippets, 

“People Also Ask” boxes, voice assistants. Goal: get extracted as the answer. 

•  GEO (Generative Engine Optimization): optimizing to be cited or paraphrased inside AI -generated 

responses from ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews, and similar tools. Goal: get chosen as a source 
when an AI synthesizes its answer. 

The three are additive, not competing. A platform that treats them as one continuous visibility problem — 
rather than three separate tools — is the product opportunity. 

2.2 Why bundle them into one product 

GEO and AEO performance leans heavily on the same authority, structure, and relevance signals traditional SEO 
already measures. A unified product's core defensibility is correlating all three signal types against the same 
content and competitive set.

The competitive set splits into legacy SEO suites now bolting on AI-visibility features, and AI-native GEO/AEO 
startups that lack deep traditional SEO tooling. No major player currently offers a genuinely unified, affordably -
priced product spanning all three layers plus content generation.

4. Target Users

agencies — with an explicit roadmap to in-house marketing teams at mid/large companies once the product has

4.1 Phase 1 personas

4.3 Business model clarification 

Plutoscope is a single-vendor SaaS, not a white-label/reseller product. “Consultant” and “Agency” above 
describe pricing tiers for external customers who manage multiple client projects under their own account — 
they are not licensees rebranding or reselling Plutoscope as their own product. Plutoscope itself is the one 
company operating the platform, with its own internal admin layer able to access any customer account for 
support and troubleshooting (see Part III §18.1 for the impersonation architecture this requires). 

5. Core Product: Four Pillars 

The product spans four capabilities: monitoring, auditing, generation, and competitive analysis, sharing a 
common data layer so insights compound across features. 

5.1 Tracking & Monitoring Dashboard 

•  Keyword and prompt-based tracking across Google rankings (SEO), featured snippets/voice answers (AEO), 

and citation frequency across AI engines (GEO). 

•  Historical trend charts per engine, with alerts on significant visibility drops or gains. 

•  Share-of-voice score blending SEO rank, AEO answer capture, and GEO citation rate into one visibility index. 

5.2 Content Audit & Optimization 

•  Technical SEO checks via existing crawling APIs. 

•  AEO readiness scoring: content structured for snippet/voice extraction. 

•  GEO readiness scoring: factual clarity, citations/sources, schema markup, freshness signals.  

5.3 Content Generation 

•  Generates content briefs from tracked topic/prompt gaps. 

•  Drafts FAQ and structured-answer blocks formatted for AEO/GEO extraction. 

•  Human-in-the-loop by design: drafts and briefs, not auto-published content. 

5.4 Competitive & Citation Analysis 

•  Competitor citation share across AI engines for shared prompt/topic sets. 

•  Gap analysis: topics competitors are cited for that the user is not. 

•  Source-of-truth tracing where visible.

Technical Feasibility: AI Citation Data 

Findings on how to actually get GEO/AEO citation data, and what that means for MVP scope.

10. Bottom Line 

Data access is better than the original plan assumed for two of the three MVP engines, but materially riskier for 
the third — and that third one (Google AI Overviews) is the single most important engine to track, since AI 
Overviews now trigger on roughly half of Google searches. 

•  ChatGPT and Perplexity both expose citations as official, first-party API data. Low cost, low legal risk, build 

on these immediately. 

•  Gemini's API also exposes official grounding citations, and can serve as a compliant proxy for “Google's AI -

grounded answers” generally. 

•  Google AI Overviews has no official third-party API. Google sued a major SERP API provider (SerpApi) in 
December 2025 over unlawful scraping, while also deploying JS challenges (“SearchGuard”) that raise 
DMCA exposure for anyone who circumvents them. 

•  Recommendation: build the MVP on the three low-risk official sources first (OpenAI, Perplexity, Gemini), 

treat Google AI Overview tracking as a separate, explicitly flagged phase -2 decision with vendor risk 
disclosed to users.

12. Why This Changes  the MVP Scope 

The original plan's MVP (Part I §6.1) listed “Google rankings + 3 major AI engines (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI 
Overviews)” as if all  three AI engines were equally buildable. They're not. ChatGPT and Perplexity are safe, well-
documented, first-party integrations available today. Google AI Overviews is the one competitors already track, 
but they do so by taking on scraping risk that just escalated. 

This doesn't kill the product, but it changes how to approach Google AI Overviews: as a deliberate, disclosed, 
vendor-outsourced risk, not a first-party build. 

13. Revised MVP Recommendation 

13.1 Build first (low risk, official APIs) 

•  ChatGPT citation tracking via OpenAI's Responses API web_search tool. 

•  Perplexity citation tracking via the Sonar API. 

•  Gemini citation tracking via Google's own grounding API. 

13.2 Add deliberately (elevated risk, vendor-mediated)

•  Build the data layer so this source can be swapped or removed without a re -architecture. 

13.3 Defer 

•  Bing Copilot and other engines — not evaluated yet.

16. System Architecture (high level) 

Four logical layers: a scheduler that runs tracked prompts against each engine's API, a normalizer that extracts 
citations/rankings into a common schema, a scoring/analysis layer, and the application layer that reads from 
that data. 

[Scheduler/Cron] -> [Engine Adapters: OpenAI | Perplexity | Gemini | Google SERP]  -> [Normalizer] -> 
[Postgres: citations, rankings, scores] -> [App/API layer] -> [Dashboard, Reports, Content Generator] 

Each engine adapter is isolated so engines can be added, removed, or swapped without touching the scoring or 
app layers.

17.3 Scalability Triggers & Migration Path 

The stack is designed to change incrementally, triggered by specific signals rather than a fixed calendar date:  

•  Dashboard reads start contending with tracking-run write volume (sustained high DB CPU) → add a 

Supabase read replica; split dashboard/report reads from ingestion writes. 

•  Scheduled tracking runs exceed what a simple cron dispatcher can complete in its window (roughly tens of 
thousands of runs/day) → move from Postgres cron/Edge Functions to a dedicated job queue (Inngest or 
Trigger.dev) with concurrency control. 

•  Multiple tracked accounts query overlapping (engine, prompt) pairs (competitors tracking each other, 

popular shared topics) → add a Redis cache (Upstash) to serve recent identical queries from cache instead 
of re-billing the AI APIs — this directly shrinks the largest variable cost line. 

•  Raw API response storage bloats the primary database → archive raw_response payloads to object storage 

(Supabase Storage/S3), keep only normalized Citation rows in Postgres. 

•  A single large customer's usage threatens to degrade service for others → introduce per-tenant rate 

limiting and usage-based metering (also needed for Enterprise billing). 

•  Compliance asks appear from Enterprise prospects (SOC2/HIPAA/BAA) → move to Supabase 

Team/Enterprise; consider dedicated/isolated database instances for the largest or most sensitive 
customers. 

 
•  Serverless costs grow faster than traffic value → revisit Vercel's committed-use Enterprise pricing, or move 

the heaviest scheduled workloads off pay-per-invocation functions onto reserved compute where 
sustained utilization is cheaper.

18.1 Admin & Support Access (Impersonation) 

Because Plutoscope is a single-vendor SaaS with a central team supporting every customer account (not a white -
label product resold by independent agencies), the platform needs a built-in way for staff to access any 
customer's account for troubleshooting — without ever needing a customer's password. 

•  StaffUser accounts live in a separate auth realm from customer Account records, so a support/engineering 

permission bypass can never be confused with or accidentally granted to a customer login.  

•  “Login as” works by minting a scoped, time-limited session for the target account server-side (using the 

Supabase service-role key, never exposed client-side) — not by knowing or resetting the customer's actual 
credentials. 

•  Every impersonation event writes an ImpersonationSession row automatically: who, which account, when, 

and why (a reason/ticket reference) — this is the audit trail, not optional logging. 

•  Row Level Security policies should be designed from Week 1 (Part VIII §43) to include a verified -staff-
session bypass path via a security-definer function, rather than relying solely on auth.uid() checks — 
retrofitting an admin bypass into RLS after the fact is materially more error-prone than designing for it up 
front. 

•  UX requirement: an unmissable persistent banner during impersonation ("Viewing as [Account Name] — 
Admin Session — End Session"), and sessions auto-expire (e.g., 30-60 minutes) rather than persisting 
indefinitely. 

•  Default to read-only access during impersonation, with an explicit, separately-logged elevated mode 

required for any write action — this limits blast radius if a staff session is ever compromised. 

Open question: Whether to disclose impersonation events to customers (e.g., a visible entry in their own 
account activity log) is a trust/compliance decision worth making deliberately — some SaaS products show 
this transparently, others treat routine support access as undisclosed. Decide before this goes into the Terms 
of Service or Privacy Policy, and before Milestone 5 builds the admin console (Part VIII §48).
